# Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association

## Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects

Adopted by the 18th WMA General Assembly, Helsinki, Finland, June 1964, and amended:

at the 29th WMA General Assembly, Tokyo, Japan, October 1975.

at the 35th WMA General Assembly, Venice, Italy, October 1983.

at the 41st WMA General Assembly, Hong Kong, September 1989.

at the 48th WMA General Assembly, Somerset West, Republic of South Africa, October 1996.

at the 52nd WMA General Assembly, Edinburgh, Scotland, October 2000.

at the 53rd WMA General Assembly, Washington, DC, USA, October 2002 (note of clarification added).

at the 55th WMA General Assembly, Tokyo, Japan, October 2004 (note of clarification added).

at the 59th WMA General Assembly, Seoul, Republic of Korea, October 2008.

at the 64th WMA General Assembly, Fortaleza, Brazil, October 2013.

and at the 75th WMA General Assembly, Helsinki, Finland, October 2024.

## Preamble

1. The World Medical Association (WMA) has developed the Declaration of Helsinki as a set of ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects, including research using biological materials or data that allow identification of the person from whom they were obtained.

The text of the Declaration is intended to be used as an integrated document, and each individual provision should be applied with consideration of all other applicable provisions.

2. Although the Declaration was adopted by physicians, the WMA believes that its principles should be followed by all organizations, groups, and individuals involved in the conduct of medical research, since these principles lay the foundation for respect for and protection of all research participants, including both patients and healthy volunteers.

## General Principles

3. The Declaration of Geneva of the WMA defines the physician’s duty in the following words: “The health and well-being of my patient will be my first consideration,” and the WMA International Code of Medical Ethics states: “The physician must commit to the primacy of patient health and well-being and must offer care in the patient’s best interest.”

4. It is the duty of the physician to promote and safeguard the health, well-being, and rights of patients, including those who participate in medical research. The physician’s knowledge and conscience must be devoted to the fulfillment of this duty.

5. Medical progress is based on research that ultimately requires the involvement of participants.

Even well-proven interventions must continually be evaluated through research for their safety, effectiveness, efficiency, accessibility, and quality.

6. Medical research involving human subjects must be conducted in accordance with ethical standards that ensure respect for all participants and protection of their health and rights.

Since medical research is conducted in the context of various forms of structural inequality, researchers must carefully consider the distribution of benefits, risks, and burdens.

Meaningful engagement with potential and enrolled participants and their communities should occur before, during, and after completion of medical research. Researchers should enable potential and enrolled participants and their communities to express their priorities and values, to influence the design and conduct of the research and other research-related activities, and to participate in the interpretation and dissemination of results.

7. The primary purpose of medical research involving human subjects is to generate knowledge for understanding the causes, development mechanisms, and consequences of diseases; to improve preventive, diagnostic, and therapeutic interventions; and ultimately to improve the health of individuals and populations.

These purposes must never take precedence over the rights and interests of individual research participants.

8. Although public health emergencies may create an urgent need for new knowledge and interventions, it remains important during such emergencies to adhere to the ethical principles set out in this Declaration.

9. It is the duty of physicians involved in medical research to protect the life, health, dignity, integrity, autonomy, privacy, and confidentiality of personal data of research participants. Responsibility for the protection of research participants must always rest with physicians or other researchers and never with the research participants, even if they have given consent.

10. Physicians and other researchers must consider the ethical and legal norms and standards for research involving human subjects that apply in the country or countries where the research was initiated and where it is to be conducted, as well as relevant international norms and standards. No national or international ethical or legal requirement should reduce or eliminate any of the protections for research participants set forth in this Declaration.

11. Medical research should be designed and conducted in a manner that avoids or minimizes harm to the environment and strives for environmental sustainability.

12. Medical research involving human subjects must be conducted only by individuals with appropriate ethical and scientific training, education, and qualifications. Such research requires supervision by a competent and appropriately qualified physician or other researcher.

Scientific integrity is of great importance in the conduct of medical research involving human subjects. The organizations, groups, and individuals involved must never permit misconduct in the conduct of research.

13. Groups that are underrepresented in medical research should be provided appropriate access to participation.

14. Physicians who combine medical research with medical care should involve their patients in research only to the extent that this is justified by its potential preventive, diagnostic, or therapeutic value, and if the physician has good reason to believe that participation in the research will not adversely affect the health of the patients who serve as research participants.

15. Research participants who are harmed as a result of participating in research must be guaranteed appropriate compensation and treatment.

## Risks, Burdens, and Benefits

16. In medical practice and medical research, most interventions involve risks and burdens.

Medical research involving human subjects may be conducted only when the importance of the research objective outweighs the risks and burdens to the research participants.

17. Every medical research study involving human subjects must be preceded by careful comparison of the possible risks and burdens to the individuals or groups included in the research with the expected benefits for those individuals or groups and for other individuals or groups affected by the condition under study.

Measures must be taken to minimize risks and burdens. Risks and burdens must be continuously monitored, assessed, and documented by the researcher.

18. Physicians and other researchers must not engage in research involving human subjects unless they are confident that the risks and burdens have been adequately assessed and can be satisfactorily managed.

When the risks and burdens are found to outweigh the expected benefits, or when there is conclusive evidence of certain outcomes, physicians and other researchers must assess whether the study should be continued, modified, or immediately stopped.

## Vulnerable Individuals, Groups, and Communities

19. Some individuals, groups, and communities, as research participants, are in a more vulnerable position due to permanent or situational and changing factors, and therefore face an increased risk of unjust treatment or harm. If such individuals, groups, and communities have health needs specific to them, their exclusion from medical research may potentially perpetuate or worsen inequality. Therefore, the harm of exclusion should be assessed and weighed against the harm of inclusion. To ensure that their inclusion in research is fair and responsible, such participants must receive specially designed support and protection.

20. Medical research involving individuals, groups, and communities in particularly vulnerable situations is justified only if it responds to their health needs and priorities and if it is expected that these individuals, groups, and communities will benefit from the knowledge, practices, or interventions obtained. Researchers should include those in particularly vulnerable situations only when the research cannot be conducted in a less vulnerable group or community, or when their exclusion may contribute to the continuation or worsening of inequality.

## Scientific Requirements and Research Protocols

21. Medical research involving human subjects must have a scientifically sound and rigorous design and execution, enabling the generation of reliable, valid, and useful knowledge and avoiding waste. Research must conform to generally accepted scientific principles and be based on thorough knowledge of the scientific literature, other relevant sources of information, adequate laboratory research, and, where appropriate, animal experimentation.

Humane treatment must be ensured for animals used in research.

22. The design and procedure of all medical research involving human subjects must be clearly described and justified in a research protocol.

The protocol must address the ethical aspects of the study and contain information on how compliance with the principles of this Declaration is ensured. The protocol must include information on the objectives, methods, expected benefits, potential risks and burdens, the researcher’s qualifications, sources of funding, any possible conflicts of interest, provisions for the protection of privacy and confidentiality, methods of incentivizing participants, provisions for treatment and/or compensation for participants in the event of injury resulting from participation, and information on any other significant aspects of the research.

In clinical research, the protocol must also describe all arrangements that apply after the end of the study.

## Research Ethics Committees

23. Before the study begins, the protocol must be submitted for consideration, comment, guidance, and approval to the appropriate research ethics committee. The work of such a committee must be transparent; the committee must be independent and have sufficient authority to resist undue influence from the researcher, sponsor, or other parties. The committee must have resources sufficient to fulfill its duties, and its members and staff collectively must have the education, training, qualifications, and diversity necessary for the effective assessment of each type of research they review.

The committee must be well acquainted with local conditions and context and must include at least one member of the public. It must consider the ethical and legal norms and standards of the country or countries in which the research is planned, as well as relevant international norms and standards, which, however, must not reduce or eliminate the protections for research participants established by this Declaration.

In international collaborative research, the research protocol must be approved by ethics committees both in the sponsor’s country and in the countries where the research is conducted.

The committee must have the authority to monitor research, recommend amendments, withdraw approval, and temporarily suspend ongoing studies. When monitoring is required, the researcher must provide the committee and/or the competent organization monitoring data and safety with the necessary information, especially information about any serious adverse events. No amendments to the protocol may be implemented without review and approval by the committee. After completion of the study, researchers must submit a final report to the committee containing a summary of the study results and conclusions.

## Privacy and Confidentiality

24. Every precaution must be taken to protect the privacy of research participants and the confidentiality of their personal data.

## Voluntary Informed Consent

25. Voluntary informed consent is an essential component of respect for personal autonomy. Participation in medical research by individuals capable of giving informed consent must be voluntary. Although consultation with relatives or community representatives may be appropriate, no individual capable of giving informed consent may be enrolled in research unless that individual has given their own voluntary consent.

26. In medical research involving human subjects capable of giving informed consent, each potential participant must receive sufficient information, presented in an accessible form, about the objectives, methods, expected benefits, potential risks and burdens, the researcher’s qualifications, sources of funding, any possible conflicts of interest, provisions for the protection of privacy and confidentiality, methods of incentivizing participants, provisions for treatment and/or compensation for participants in the event of injury resulting from participation in the study, and information on any other significant aspects of the research.

The potential participant must be informed of the right to refuse to participate in the study or to withdraw consent to participate at any time without any adverse consequences. Particular attention must be paid to the specific informational and communication needs of each potential participant and to the methods used to provide information.

After ensuring that the potential participant has understood the information provided, the physician or another qualified professional must obtain the potential participant’s voluntary informed consent, documented in paper or electronic form. If consent cannot be expressed in paper or electronic form, oral consent must be properly witnessed and documented.

All participants in medical research must be given the opportunity to receive information about the general conclusions and results of the study.

27. When obtaining informed consent for participation in research, the physician or other researcher must exercise particular caution in cases where the potential participant is dependent on the physician or researcher or may consent under pressure. In such cases, informed consent must be obtained by a person who has appropriate qualifications and on whom the potential participant is not dependent.

28. In medical research involving individuals who are unable to give voluntary informed consent, the physician or another qualified professional must obtain informed consent from the legal representative, taking into account the preferences and values expressed by the potential participant.

Those who are unable to give voluntary informed consent are in a particularly vulnerable position and are entitled to appropriate protective measures. In addition to receiving protection as persons in a particularly vulnerable situation, individuals unable to consent should be included in research only if there is reason to believe that the research will either directly benefit them or involves only minimal risks and burdens.

29. If a potential research participant who is unable to give voluntary informed consent is capable of expressing their own opinion about participation in the study, the physician or another qualified professional must seek that opinion in addition to the consent of the legal representative, taking into account the preferences and values expressed by the potential participant. The objection of the potential participant must be taken into account.

30. Research involving individuals who are physically or mentally incapable of giving consent, for example patients who are unconscious, may be conducted only if the physical or mental condition that prevents the giving of informed consent is an essential characteristic of the research group. In such cases, the physician or another qualified professional must seek informed consent from the legal representative. If such a representative is not available, and if the research cannot be delayed, the research may proceed without informed consent, provided that the specific reasons for including participants with a condition that prevents the giving of informed consent are stated in the research protocol and that the research has been approved by a research ethics committee.

As soon as possible, voluntary informed consent for continued participation in the study must be obtained from the legal representative or from the participant if the participant regains the capacity to provide consent.

31. The physician or other researcher must provide potential participants with complete information about which aspects of treatment are related to the research being conducted. A patient’s refusal to participate in research or decision to withdraw from research must never adversely affect the patient-physician relationship or the provision of standard medical care.

32. Physicians or other qualified professionals must obtain the voluntary informed consent of research participants for the collection, processing, storage, and intended secondary use of biological material and data that identify or may identify a person. Any collection and storage of data or biological material from research participants for multiple and indefinite use must comply with the requirements set forth in the WMA Declaration of Taipei, including respect for individual rights and the principles of governance. The research ethics committee must approve the establishment and monitor the ongoing use of such databases and biobanks.

Where obtaining consent is impossible or impracticable, secondary research on stored data or biological material may be conducted only after review and approval by a research ethics committee.

## Use of Placebo

33. The benefits, risks, burdens, and effectiveness of a new intervention must be tested against those of the best proven interventions, except in the following circumstances:

- when no proven intervention exists, the use of placebo or no intervention is acceptable; or

- when, for compelling and scientifically sound methodological reasons, the use of any intervention other than the best proven one, the use of placebo, or no intervention is necessary to determine the efficacy or safety of an intervention, and participants receiving any intervention other than the best proven one, placebo, or no intervention will not be subject to additional risks of serious or irreversible harm as a result of not receiving the best proven intervention.

Extreme care must be taken to avoid abuse of this option.

## Post-Trial Provisions

34. Before a clinical trial begins, sponsors and researchers must make provisions for post-trial access, whether provided by themselves, healthcare systems, or governments, for all participants who still need an intervention that has been found in the trial to be beneficial and sufficiently safe. Exceptions to this requirement must be approved by a research ethics committee. Specific information about post-trial access must be disclosed to participants during the informed consent process.

## Research Registration, Publication, and Dissemination of Results

35. Medical research involving human subjects must be registered in a publicly accessible database before the first participant is enrolled.

36. Researchers, authors, sponsors, editors, and publishers have ethical obligations regarding the publication and dissemination of research results. Researchers are obliged to ensure public access to the results of their research involving human subjects and are responsible for the timeliness, completeness, and accuracy of their reports. All parties must follow generally accepted ethical principles in preparing research reports. Positive, negative, and inconclusive results must be published or otherwise made publicly available. Publications must disclose sources of funding, institutional affiliations, and existing conflicts of interest. Reports of research that does not conform to the principles set forth in this Declaration should not be accepted for publication.

## Unproven Interventions in Clinical Practice

37. If, in order to restore health or alleviate suffering in an individual patient, an unproven intervention is used because approved options are inadequate or ineffective and enrollment in a clinical trial is not possible, that intervention must subsequently become the subject of research designed to evaluate its efficacy and safety. Physicians involved in such interventions must first seek expert advice, assess possible risks, burdens, and benefits, and obtain informed consent. They must also document and, when required, share data, and must not compromise clinical trials. Such interventions must never be used to circumvent the requirements for the protection of research participants established by this Declaration.
